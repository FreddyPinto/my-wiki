The Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM) model is the standard mathematical framework of modern cosmology that describes how the Universe evolves. It explains everything from the Big Bang and cosmic expansion to the formation of galaxies. [1, 2, 3] 
The name refers to its two primary components:

* Λ (Lambda): Represents dark energy, a mysterious force causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate.
* CDM: Stands for Cold Dark Matter, slow-moving, invisible matter that acts as a gravitational scaffold for stars and galaxies to form. [4, 5, 6] 

## Core Principles

* Expansion & Acceleration: The Universe began with the Big Bang and is expanding. While gravity pulls matter inward, Λ (dark energy) pushes the universe outward at an ever-increasing rate. [1, 4, 5, 7, 8] 
* The Cosmic Recipe: The model estimates the total mass-energy budget of the Universe to be roughly 5% normal matter (stars and gas), 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy. [9] 
* Predictive Power: Despite having only a few free adjustable parameters, ΛCDM accurately predicts the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, the large-scale web-like distribution of galaxies, and the abundance of light elements. [10, 11]

## Limitations and Current Research
While ΛCDM is the most widely accepted cosmological framework, it is not without challenges. Cosmologists have identified a few anomalies: [12] 

* The "Hubble Tension": Discrepancies exist when measuring how fast the universe is expanding depending on whether scientists look at the early universe (via the CMB) or the local universe (via supernovae). [8, 10, 13, 14] 
* Dark Sector Unknowns: The model uses Λ and CDM as placeholders, but physicists still lack a fundamental understanding of what dark energy and dark matter are actually made of. [4, 13] 
* Galaxy Formation: On small scales, the model's predictions sometimes clash with the actual density and erratic movements observed in certain dwarf galaxies. [15]
