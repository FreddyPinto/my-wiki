# Big Bang

## Definition

The Big Bang is the prevailing cosmological model for the origin and early evolution of the Universe. It describes the Universe as beginning approximately 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense initial state and expanding and cooling ever since. Within the Lambda-CDM framework, the Big Bang marks the starting point from which cosmic expansion, the formation of matter, and the emergence of large-scale structure all follow.

## Key Characteristics

- Represents the origin event of the observable Universe; all time, space, matter, and energy emerged from this initial state.
- Followed by rapid expansion and cooling that allowed quarks to form protons and neutrons, then light nuclei (Big Bang nucleosynthesis), and eventually neutral atoms ~380,000 years later (producing the CMB).
- The Lambda-CDM model accurately predicts the primordial abundances of light elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium) produced during Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
- The ongoing expansion of the Universe is the continuing consequence of the Big Bang, now accelerated by dark energy.

## Applications

- Provides the temporal and physical starting conditions for all modern cosmological models and simulations.
- Big Bang nucleosynthesis predictions are tested against observed elemental abundances, providing a consistency check on the standard model.
- Motivates research into the very early Universe (inflation, baryogenesis) that lies before or just after the Big Bang.
