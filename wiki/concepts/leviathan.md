# Leviathan

## Definition

*Leviathan* (full title: *Leviathan, or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil*) is a 1651 book by the English philosopher [Thomas Hobbes](../entities/thomas-hobbes.md). It is one of the earliest and most influential works of modern political philosophy, providing a systematic materialist account of human nature and a justification for absolute sovereign authority grounded in social contract theory.

## Key Characteristics

- Published in 1651, during the turmoil of the English Civil War.
- Argues from materialist premises that humans are fundamentally self-interested and that life in a "state of nature" — without government — would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."
- Derives the social contract as a rational necessity: individuals surrender their natural freedom to an absolute sovereign (the "Leviathan") in exchange for security and peace.
- The sovereign's authority is near-absolute in Hobbes's account, contrasting sharply with Locke's later, more limited conception of government.
- The title refers to the Leviathan of the Hebrew Bible — a sea monster symbolising a powerful, awe-inspiring force — used as a metaphor for the commonwealth.

## Applications

- Foundational text for the social contract tradition in political philosophy.
- Directly influenced subsequent contract theorists including John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who responded to and revised Hobbes's framework.
- Continues to be a central text in political philosophy, international relations theory (realism), and the philosophy of law.
