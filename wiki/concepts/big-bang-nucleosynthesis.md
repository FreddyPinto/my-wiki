# Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

## Definition

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) is the process by which the lightest atomic nuclei — primarily hydrogen, helium-4, deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7 — were synthesised from protons and neutrons during the first few minutes after the Big Bang, when the Universe was hot and dense enough for nuclear fusion reactions to occur. It is one of the key observational tests of the Big Bang model and the Lambda-CDM framework.

## Key Characteristics

- Occurred approximately 10 seconds to 20 minutes after the Big Bang, when temperatures fell from ~10 billion K to ~1 billion K.
- Produced a Universe composed of roughly 75% hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass, with trace amounts of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7.
- The predicted elemental abundances match observations of the oldest, least-processed astrophysical environments, providing strong evidence for the Big Bang model.
- The Lambda-CDM model accurately predicts these primordial abundances, making BBN one of its three major observational pillars (alongside the CMB and large-scale structure).
- The baryon-to-photon ratio inferred from BBN is consistent with the value measured independently from the CMB power spectrum.

## Applications

- Provides an independent measurement of the total baryon density of the Universe.
- Used to constrain the number of light neutrino species and other extensions to the Standard Model of particle physics.
- Observations of metal-poor stars and distant quasar absorption systems are used to measure primordial elemental abundances and test BBN predictions.
