# Parallel Lives

## Definition

*Parallel Lives* (Greek: *Βίοι Παράλληλοι*, Latin: *Vitae Parallelae*) is a series of biographies by the Greek historian and essayist [Plutarch](../entities/plutarch.md), written in the early 2nd century AD. Each biography pairs a famous Greek with a famous Roman of comparable career or character — Alexander the Great with Julius Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero, and so on — and typically ends with a comparative essay (synkrisis) assessing the two figures. It is one of the most important surviving works of ancient biography and a primary source for the history of Greece and Rome.

## Key Characteristics

- Comprises 23 surviving paired biographies (46 individual lives) plus 4 unpaired lives.
- Written primarily as moral instruction: Plutarch's goal was to illuminate character and virtue through the study of great men, not to produce strict historical chronicles.
- Includes the *Life of Theseus*, which is the primary ancient source for the preservation of the Ship of Theseus and the thought experiment that bears his name.
- Written in Greek; the standard English translations are by John Dryden (revised by Arthur Hugh Clough) and by Robin Waterfield (Oxford World's Classics).

## Applications

- Primary ancient source for many events in Greek and Roman history, widely used by Renaissance humanists and playwrights (including Shakespeare).
- The *Life of Theseus* is the direct origin of the Ship of Theseus paradox in philosophical literature.
