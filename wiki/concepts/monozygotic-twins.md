# Monozygotic Twins

Monozygotic twins (colloquially, identical twins) are individuals who develop from a single fertilized egg (zygote) that divides into two separate embryos shortly after fertilization. Because both embryos originate from the same egg and sperm, they share essentially identical genomes and are always the same biological sex.

## Core Characteristics

- **Origin**: One egg fertilized by one sperm → one zygote → splits into two embryos.
- **Genetic similarity**: ~100% shared DNA, in contrast to dizygotic (fraternal) twins who share ~50%.
- **Sex**: Always the same sex, because sex is determined genetically at fertilization.
- **Occurrence rate**: A random embryological event in approximately 3–4 per 1,000 births worldwide.
- **Physical uniqueness**: Genetic identity does not guarantee physical identity. Epigenetic changes and differing intrauterine environments produce unique fingerprints and minor physical disparities between the twins.

## Contrast with Dizygotic Twins

Dizygotic (fraternal) twins result from two separately fertilized eggs. They share approximately 50% of their genes — the same proportion as non-twin siblings — and may be different sexes. Monozygotic twins are the only naturally occurring case of near-perfect genetic identity between two distinct individuals.

## Relevance to Personal Identity

Monozygotic twins are a direct empirical challenge to purely genetic accounts of personal identity. Two individuals can begin with identical genomes yet develop distinct personalities, memories, and physical traits. This shows that identity is not fully determined by genetics: [epigenetics](../concepts/epigenetics.md), environment, and lived experience are constitutive of what makes a person who they are. This reinforces the view in [Personal Identity](../concepts/personal-identity.md) that psychological continuity and environmental history — not just biological substrate — ground the self.

## Research Value

Because genetic variables are held essentially constant, monozygotic twins are the primary tool in scientific research for isolating the effects of environment (nurture) from genetics (nature). Differences between monozygotic twin pairs are attributed to environmental or epigenetic factors, not inherited DNA variation.
