# Cosmic Inflation

## Definition

Cosmic inflation is a theoretical period of extremely rapid, exponential expansion of the Universe that is hypothesised to have occurred in the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang (approximately 10⁻³⁶ to 10⁻³² seconds). It was proposed to resolve several otherwise unexplained features of the observable Universe, including its large-scale uniformity, spatial flatness, and the absence of magnetic monopoles.

## Key Characteristics

- Proposed by Alan Guth in 1980 as an extension to the standard Big Bang model.
- Postulates that a brief period of exponential expansion caused the Universe to grow by a factor of at least 10²⁶ in a tiny fraction of a second.
- Explains the near-perfect uniformity of the Cosmic Microwave Background: regions of the Universe that appear causally disconnected today were in causal contact before inflation.
- Predicts that quantum fluctuations during inflation were stretched to cosmic scales, seeding the density perturbations that grew into galaxies and large-scale structure.
- Inflation is not yet part of the core Lambda-CDM model but is widely assumed as the mechanism generating the initial conditions that ΛCDM subsequently evolves.
- Direct observational confirmation of inflation would require detection of primordial gravitational waves in the CMB's B-mode polarisation.

## Applications

- Sets the initial conditions (flat geometry, scale-invariant spectrum of density fluctuations) that the Lambda-CDM model takes as inputs.
- Motivates CMB polarisation experiments (e.g., BICEP/Keck, LiteBIRD) searching for the inflationary gravitational wave signal.
- Connects cosmology to particle physics: the inflaton field driving inflation is an active area of theoretical research.
