# Epigenetics

Epigenetics is the study of heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve alterations to the underlying DNA sequence. Where genetics asks *what* genes an organism has, epigenetics asks *how* and *when* those genes are switched on or off — changes that can be influenced by environment, behavior, and development, and that can persist across cell divisions.

## Core Mechanism

Epigenetic regulation operates through chemical modifications to DNA or the histone proteins around which DNA is wound. Common mechanisms include:

- **DNA methylation**: addition of a methyl group to a cytosine base, typically silencing the associated gene.
- **Histone modification**: chemical tags (acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation) that alter how tightly DNA is coiled around histones, affecting accessibility for transcription.
- **Non-coding RNA**: regulatory RNA molecules that modulate gene expression without being translated into protein.

These modifications change which genes are expressed in a given cell or at a given time — without changing the DNA sequence itself.

## Relevance to Monozygotic Twins

[Monozygotic twins](../concepts/monozygotic-twins.md) begin with essentially identical genomes, yet epigenetic divergence accumulates over their lifetimes in response to differing environments, diets, stresses, and experiences. This is the primary explanation for why identical twins can develop different fingerprints, disease susceptibilities, and physical characteristics despite sharing the same DNA. Epigenetics is therefore the mechanism by which environment writes itself onto genetic identity.

## Relevance to Personal Identity

Epigenetic divergence in monozygotic twins provides empirical grounding for the view that personal identity is not reducible to genetics. It supports the position explored in [Personal Identity](../concepts/personal-identity.md) that the environment and lived history — not just inherited biological substrate — are constitutive of who a person is.
