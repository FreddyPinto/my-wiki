# Social Contract Theory

## Definition

Social contract theory is a tradition in political philosophy that grounds the legitimacy of political authority and the obligations of citizens in a (real or hypothetical) agreement — a "contract" — among individuals or between individuals and a governing body. Rather than deriving political authority from divine right or natural hierarchy, social contract theorists argue that legitimate government arises from the consent of the governed, who agree to surrender some freedoms in exchange for the protection and benefits of organised society.

## Key Characteristics

- Treats political authority as artificial and contingent — it exists because people collectively agree (or would agree under certain conditions) to create it.
- Distinguishes between the "state of nature" (the condition of humanity without government) and civil society; the contract explains the transition between the two.
- Different theorists describe the state of nature and the resulting contract very differently, leading to very different conclusions about the scope of legitimate authority.
- Major contributors include Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, each developing a distinct version of the theory.

## Key Variants

- **Hobbesian contract** ([Thomas Hobbes](../entities/thomas-hobbes.md)): The state of nature is a "war of all against all"; individuals surrender nearly all freedoms to an absolute sovereign in exchange for security and order.
- **Lockean contract** ([John Locke](../entities/john-locke.md)): The state of nature is governed by natural law and natural rights; individuals consent to government to better protect pre-existing rights to life, liberty, and property. Government that violates these rights forfeits its legitimacy.
- **Rousseauian contract**: The "general will" of the community is the basis of legitimate authority; sovereignty belongs to the people collectively, not to any individual or representative.

## Applications

- Foundational to liberal democratic theory, constitutionalism, and modern human rights frameworks.
- Influences debates in political philosophy about the justification of the state, civil disobedience, and distributive justice.
- Revived in contemporary political philosophy by John Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971), whose "veil of ignorance" thought experiment is a neo-contractarian device.
