# Theseus's Paradox

[Theseus's Paradox.md](../../raw/Theseus's Paradox.md)

## Summary

The Ship of Theseus is an ancient philosophical thought experiment about identity and change. It asks whether an object that has had all of its components replaced over time is still fundamentally the same object.

## The Two-Stage Paradox

The paradox unfolds in two stages:

1. **Gradual replacement**: If the wooden planks of Theseus's ship are replaced one by one until none of the originals remain, is it still the same ship?
2. **Hobbes's twist**: If someone collects all the discarded original planks and reassembles them into a second ship, which of the two resulting ships is the *real* Ship of Theseus?

The thought experiment originates with the Greek biographer [Plutarch](../entities/plutarch.md), who described the Athenians preserving [Theseus](../entities/theseus.md)'s ship in [Athens](../entities/athens.md). The second stage was added by the 17th-century philosopher [Thomas Hobbes](../entities/thomas-hobbes.md).

## Key Entities

- [Theseus](../entities/theseus.md) — mythical king whose ship is the subject of the paradox
- [Plutarch](../entities/plutarch.md) — Greek philosopher and biographer who documented the ship's preservation
- [Thomas Hobbes](../entities/thomas-hobbes.md) — 17th-century philosopher who added the rebuilt-ship twist
- [Athens](../entities/athens.md) — city that preserved the ship

## Applications

The paradox extends beyond objects to three main domains:

- **Metaphysics**: Questions whether identity is grounded in physical material (matter) or form, design, and history (spatiotemporal continuity).
- **Personal identity**: Biologically, the cells in the human body are constantly replaced, yet we consider ourselves the same person over time.
- **Organizations and brands**: Companies replace employees, executives, and processes — at what point does an institution lose its original identity?

## Philosophical Positions

| Position | Core Claim |
|---|---|
| Spatiotemporal Continuity | Ship A (the gradually repaired ship) is the real ship; it maintains continuous existence in time and space. The rebuilt ship is merely a replica. |
| Constitutionalism | An object is defined by both its current matter and its history; both ships have valid but distinct claims to being the "real" ship. |
| Perdurantism | An object should be viewed as a four-dimensional entity stretching across time; "The Ship of Theseus" is the entire collection of its states from start to finish. |
